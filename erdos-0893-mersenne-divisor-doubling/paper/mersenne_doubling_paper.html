DIVISORS OF MERSENNE NUMBERS UNDER DOUBLING, AND PRIME FACTORS OF 2p − 1 BESIDE NEAR-WAGSTAFF NUMBERS: SHARP ELEMENTARY BOUNDS, EXACT REDUCTIONS, AND BARRIERS

[AUTHOR NAME]

ABSTRACT. Let f(n) = ∑k≤n τ(2k − 1). Erdős asked whether f(2n)/f(n) tends to a limit (Problem #893). Kovač and Luca proved lim sup f(2n)/f(n) = ∞. Whether the limit is +∞ is open. We prove that f(2n) ≥ 3f(n) for every n, with equality exactly for n ∈ {1, 2, 4}. If the Fermat number Fa is composite, then f(2n) ≥ (4 − 16/(3·2a−2 + 4)) f(n) on [2a, 2a+1). Hence f(2n) ≥ (24/7) f(n) for 32 ≤ n < 233, and lim inf f(2n)/f(n) ≥ 4 if all large Fermat numbers are composite. For every C, the set {n : f(2n) > C f(n)} meets every chain n, 2n, …, 2J(C)−1n and has lower density at least 1/(2J(C) − 1). We reduce f(2n)/f(n) → ∞ exactly to a statement about indices whose odd part has few divisors. This gives a conditional theorem whose hypothesis is far weaker than that of Kovač and Luca. We also show that one natural route to a negative answer needs primes p for which τ(2p + 1) is bounded and ω(2p − 1) = exp((log 2 + o(1)) log p / log log p).

This leads to the constant βK = lim supp: τ(2p+1) ≤ K log ω(2p − 1) · log log p / log p and the conjecture βK = 0. We do not settle it. We prove that a disproof would settle two open problems at once. We give barrier theorems, in sketch form: axioms that capture the bounded-depth properties of the factorizations are consistent with βK = 0 and with βK = ∞. We give an “honest ideal” family showing that exact divisibility at the level of ideals is still not enough. We prove the function-field analogue, which holds uniformly in p. All numerical claims were re-checked against independent factor tables. This corrects a claim in the working notes: ω(2397 − 1) = 9, not at most 8.

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 Erdős Problem #893.

Write τ for the divisor function, ω for the number of distinct prime factors, and

T(k) = τ(2k − 1),    f(n) = ∑k≤n T(k).

Erdős [6] asked whether f(2n)/f(n) tends to a limit; this is Problem #893 in [5]. He remarked that f probably has no simple asymptotic formula because it grows too fast. Kovač and Luca [10] proved lim supn f(2n)/f(n) = ∞, so no finite limit exists. They also proved that f(2n)/f(n) → ∞ under either of two conjectures. One of them is

ω(Φd(2)) ≤ 10 log d  for all sufficiently large d.     (1)

Whether f(2n)/f(n) → ∞ unconditionally, equivalently whether lim inf f(2n)/f(n) = ∞, is open. To our knowledge no lower bound for the lim inf beyond the trivial f(2n) > 2f(n) has been recorded. Part I proves the following.

Theorem A (Sharp constant; Theorems 4.4, 4.6). f(2n) ≥ 3f(n) for all n ≥ 1, with equality exactly for n ∈ {1, 2, 4}. If a ≥ 4 and Fa = 22a + 1 is composite, then f(2n) ≥ (4 − 16/(3·2a−2 + 4)) f(n) for 2a ≤ n < 2a+1. In particular f(2n) ≥ (24/7) f(n) for 32 ≤ n < 233. If Fa is composite for all large a, then lim inf f(2n)/f(n) ≥ 4.

Theorem B (Good n are everywhere; Theorem 5.2). Let D(M) = maxm≤M τ(m) and J(C) = min{J : D(2J) − 2 > J log2 C}. For every C > 1 and every n, some 0 ≤ j < J(C) has f(2j+1n) > C f(2jn). Hence {n : f(2n) > C f(n)} has lower natural density at least 1/(2J(C) − 1). Here J(C) = O(log log C · log log log C).

Theorem B reproves the theorem of Kovač and Luca, since it gives lim sup = ∞.

Theorem C (Exact reduction; Theorem 6.2, Corollary 6.3). Call k K-thin if the odd part of k has fewer than K divisors. Then f(2n)/f(n) → ∞ if and only if, for every fixed K, ∑k≤n, k K-thin T(k) = o(f(2n) − f(n)). Consequently f(2n)/f(n) → ∞ provided that, for every K, ω(Φd(2)) = o(ψ(d)/log2 d) as d → ∞ through K-thin d, where ψ(x) = maxm≤x τ(m) = 2(1+o(1)) log x / log log x.

This hypothesis is much weaker than (1). It allows ω(Φd(2)) as large as ψ(d)1−o(1), and it says nothing at all about indices that are not thin.

Theorem D (What a negative answer needs; Propositions 6.4, 6.5). If f(2n) ≤ C f(n) for infinitely many n, then for a fixed K = K(C) there are K-thin d ≤ n with ω(Φd(2)) ≥ (1 − o(1)) ψ(n)/(K log22 n) for each such n. If moreover a single prime index p ≤ n carries a fixed fraction 1/λ of f(n), then τ(2p + 1) ≤ Cλ and ω(2p − 1) ≥ exp((log 2 + o(1)) log p / log log p) for infinitely many such p.

1.2 Mersenne numbers beside near-Wagstaff numbers.

Theorem D isolates a concrete arithmetic scenario. Let p be prime, Mp = 2p − 1, and for K ≥ 4 let

PK = {p : τ(2p + 1) ≤ K},    βK = lim supp→∞, p∈PK (log ω(Mp) · log log p) / log p,

with βK := 0 if PK is finite. For p > 3, τ(2p + 1) = 4 exactly when (2p + 1)/3 is a Wagstaff prime. Theorem D shows that βK ≥ log 2 for some K is necessary for the “prime spike” route to a negative answer to Problem #893.

Conjecture 1.1. βK = 0 for every K ≥ 4.

Gillies’ heuristic [7] (see also Wagstaff [15]) predicts that ω(Mp) is typically of order log p. This would make Conjecture 1.1 true with room to spare. Unconditionally, nothing better than ω(Mp) ≤ (log 2/2 + o(1)) p / log p seems to be known pointwise. We do not resolve Conjecture 1.1. Part II proves:

  • a disproof would give infinitely many composite Mp (p prime) on which (2p + 1)/3 has at most K/2 − 1 prime factors (Theorem 8.1);
  • barrier theorems: families of fake factorizations satisfy every bounded-depth property of the true ones, yet give βK = 0 or βK = ∞ as desired, and even saturate the trivial constant log 2/2 (Section 9);
  • an “honest ideal” obstruction: elements 2k − ζp share every algebraic feature of 2 − ζp used by class-group, Stickelberger, Galois and lattice methods, but have ω ≥ τ(k) (Theorem 10.1);
  • the function-field analogue: over 𝔽ℓ[T], τ(ap + 1) ≤ K implies ω(ap − 1) ≤ deg a · (1 + log2 K), uniformly in p (Theorem 11.1);
  • a divisor-lattice inequality and an incidence lemma that isolate the one estimate every method we tried needs (Section 12).

1.3 What is new, and what is not.

Section 15 compares each result with the literature. In short, neither problem is resolved here. The theorem lim sup f(2n)/f(n) = ∞ is due to Kovač and Luca. The trivial bounds, Zsigmondy-type facts and all factor data are classical or tabulated. To our knowledge, the following are not in the literature: Theorems A–D, the barrier theorems, the honest-ideal obstruction, the function-field theorem and the divisor-lattice lemma. All of them are elementary, or use only classical analytic input (Siegel–Walfisz, Bombieri–Vinogradov). Several statements in the working notes behind this paper turned out to be false or imprecise. They are corrected in Section 14.

Remark 1.2 (Provenance). The results were first obtained in two working notes written with extensive help from AI research agents. For this paper every proof given in full was re-derived and checked line by line. Every numerical claim was re-verified against independent factor tables (Section 13). Results proved only in sketch form are labelled as such.

2. PRELIMINARIES

For odd d let o(d) = ordd 2. We use the following standard facts.

  • (P1) Cyclotomic factorization. 2k − 1 = ∏d∣k Φd(2), and 2m + 1 = ∏e∣2m, e∤m Φe(2).
  • (P2) Bang–Zsigmondy [1, 17]. For n ∉ {1, 6} there is a prime q with ordq 2 = n (a primitive prime for n). Primitive primes for distinct n are distinct. A primitive prime for n divides 2k − 1 if and only if n ∣ k.
  • (P3) Lifting the exponent. If q is an odd prime with q ∣ 2m − 1, then vq(2mt − 1) = vq(2m − 1) + vq(t).
  • (P4) Coprimality. gcd(2m − 1, 2m + 1) = 1, so T(2m) = T(m) τ(2m + 1).
  • (P5) Wigert [16]. ψ(x) := maxm≤x τ(m) = 2(1+o(1)) log x / log log x.

Lemma 2.1 (Divisor gain). Let m ∣ k. Suppose 2k − 1 has s distinct prime factors that do not divide 2m − 1. Then T(k) ≥ 2s T(m). In particular, if E is a set of divisors e of k with e ∤ m, then T(k) ≥ 2|E∖{1,6}| T(m).

Proof. τ(N) = ∏q∣N (vq(N) + 1). Every prime factor of N′ = 2m − 1 divides N = 2k − 1 to at least the same power. Each of the s new primes contributes a factor ≥ 2. For the second claim apply (P2). ∎

Lemma 2.2. T(k) ≤ (k + 1)ω(2k−1) and ω(2k − 1) ≤ τ(k) maxd∣k ω(Φd(2)).

Proof. Each exponent satisfies vq(2k − 1) ≤ log3 2k < k. The second bound follows from (P1). ∎

Lemma 2.3. (a) τ(2m + 1) ≥ 4 unless m is a power of 2 or m = 3; also τ(9) = 3. (b) If u is the odd part of m, then ω(2m + 1) ≥ τ(u) − 1.

Proof. (a) Suppose m has an odd divisor u > 1. Then 2m/u + 1 is a proper divisor of 2m + 1 greater than 1. If τ(2m + 1) = 3, then 2m + 1 = q2, so (q − 1)(q + 1) = 2m, which forces q = 3 and m = 3. (b) Write m = 2cu. By (P1) the indices e ∣ 2m with e ∤ m are e = 2c+1w with w ∣ u. There are τ(u) of them, and each except possibly e = 6 has a primitive prime by (P2). ∎

PART I. THE DOUBLING RATIO

3. AN EXACT IDENTITY

Theorem 3.1. f(n) = ∑d odd ⌊n/o(d)⌋. Hence f(2n) − 2f(n) = #{d odd : {n/o(d)} ≥ 1/2}, where {·} is the fractional part.

Proof. An odd d divides 2k − 1 if and only if o(d) ∣ k. So f(n) = ∑d #{k ≤ n : o(d) ∣ k} = ∑d ⌊n/o(d)⌋, where only finitely many d contribute. For real x, ⌊2x⌋ − 2⌊x⌋ is 1 if {x} ≥ 1/2 and 0 otherwise. ∎

The identity is elementary and probably folklore. It shows that f(2n) > 2f(n) always, since d = 3 gives {n/2} = 1/2 for odd n, and d = 2n+1 − 1 gives o(d) = n + 1 > n. It also recasts the problem: do the moduli d whose order puts n in the upper half of a period outnumber ∑d ⌊n/o(d)⌋ by an unbounded factor?

4. THE SHARP CONSTANT 3

Write Fi = 22i + 1. By (P4), T(2a+1) = τ(Fa) T(2a) and T(2a) = ∏i<a τ(Fi).

Lemma 4.1. Let r = 2bu with u > 1 odd, and C(r) = ∑i=0b T(2iu). Then C(r) ≤ T(2r)/3.

Proof. By (P4), T(2i+1u) = T(2iu) τ(22iu + 1). Since u > 1, the exponent 2iu is never a power of 2. By Lemma 2.3(a), each factor τ(22iu + 1) is ≥ 4, except the factor τ(9) = 3 when i = 0, u = 3. If all factors are ≥ 4, then T(2iu) ≤ 4−(b+1−i) T(2r), and C(r) ≤ T(2r) ∑j≥1 4−j = T(2r)/3. If u = 3, the same bound holds for i ≥ 1, and T(3) = T(6)/3 ≤ T(2r)/(3·4b). Then C(r) ≤ T(2r)(∑j=1b 4−j + 1/(3·4b)) = T(2r)/3. ∎

Lemma 4.2. For c ≥ 1, T(3·2c) ≥ (3/2)·2c T(2c).

Proof. Let N = 23·2c − 1 and N′ = 22c − 1. The indices 3·2i, 0 ≤ i ≤ c, i ≠ 1, divide 3·2c but not 2c. Their c primitive primes (P2) are distinct, do not divide N′, and differ from 3, because ord3 2 = 2. By (P3), v3(N′) = v3(42c−1 − 1) = 1 and v3(N) = 1 + v3(3·2c−1) = 2. So the prime 3 contributes 3 to τ(N) against 2 to τ(N′), and τ(N) ≥ τ(N′)·(3/2)·2c. ∎

Lemma 4.3. T(2i+1) ≥ 2T(2i). Hence ∑i<a T(2i) < 2T(2a−1) for a ≥ 1.

Proof. τ(Fi) ≥ 2. Then ∑i<a T(2i) ≤ T(2a−1) ∑j=0a−1 2−j < 2T(2a−1). ∎

The decomposition. Let R = ℤ ∩ (n/2, n]. Every m ≤ n is uniquely m = r/2j with r ∈ R and 0 ≤ j ≤ v2(r). Hence

f(n) = ∑r∈R C(r),    C(r) = ∑j=0v2(r) T(r/2j),     (2)

which agrees with Lemma 4.1 for r = 2bu. The numbers 2r, r ∈ R, are distinct elements of (n, 2n]. So f(2n) ≥ f(n) + ∑r∈R T(2r), and for every λ,

f(2n) − (λ + 1) f(n) ≥ ∑r∈R (T(2r) − λ C(r)).     (3)

Theorem 4.4. f(2n) ≥ 3f(n) for every n ≥ 1, with equality exactly for n ∈ {1, 2, 4}.

Proof. For n ≤ 15 this is a direct computation from T(1), …, T(30) = 1, 2, 2, 4, 2, 6, 2, 8, 4, 8, 4, 24, 2, 8, 8, 16, 2, 32, 2, 48, 12, 16, 4, 96, 8, 8, 8, 64, 8, 96. The ratios f(2n)/f(n) for n = 1, …, 15 are 3, 3, 3.4, 3, 3.545, 3.941, 4.053, 3.741, 4.355, 4.744, 4.953, 4.672, 4.768, 5.208, 5.941.

Let n ≥ 16 and 2a ≤ n < 2a+1, so a ≥ 4. Apply (3) with λ = 2. For r ∈ R, r ≠ 2a, Lemma 4.1 gives T(2r) − 2C(r) ≥ T(2r)/3 ≥ 0. For r = 2a, Lemma 4.3 gives

T(2a+1) − 2 ∑i≤a T(2i) > (τ(Fa) − 2) T(2a) − 4T(2a−1) ≥ −4T(2a−1).

To compensate, let r0 = 3·2a−2 if n < 3·2a−1, and r0 = 3·2a−1 otherwise. In both cases r0 ∈ R and r0 ≠ 2a. Also 23·2a−1 − 1 divides 22r0 − 1, so T(2r0) ≥ T(3·2a−1). By Lemma 4.2, (1/3)T(2r0) ≥ (1/3)T(3·2a−1) ≥ 2a−2 T(2a−1) ≥ 4T(2a−1). The sum in (3) is therefore strictly positive. ∎

Remark 4.5 (Sharpness for soft methods). The constant 3 cannot be improved by any argument that uses only the soft properties listed in Section 9.1. Consider a model in which Φ2a(2) = Fa−1 is replaced by a product of many primes, so that T*(2a) dominates f*(2a), and Fa is “prime”. Then T*(2a+1) = 2T*(2a), so f*(2a+1)/f*(2a) → 3.

Theorem 4.6. If a ≥ 4 and Fa is composite, then f(2n) ≥ (4 − 16/(3·2a−2 + 4)) f(n) for all n ∈ [2a, 2a+1).

Proof. Apply (3) with λ = 3. The terms with r ≠ 2a are ≥ 0 by Lemma 4.1. Since Fa ≡ 2 (mod 3) is not a square, compositeness gives τ(Fa) ≥ 4. With Lemma 4.3, the r = 2a term is then ≥ T(2a) − 6T(2a−1) ≥ −4T(2a−1). Put t = T(2a−1)/f(n); this gives f(2n)/f(n) ≥ 4 − 4t. On the other hand 3·2a−1 ≤ 2n, so by Lemma 4.2 f(2n) ≥ T(3·2a−1) ≥ 3·2a−2 T(2a−1), that is, f(2n)/f(n) ≥ 3·2a−2 t. The larger of the two bounds is smallest where they meet, at t = 4/(3·2a−2 + 4). ∎

Corollary 4.7. (a) F5, …, F32 are composite, so f(2n) ≥ (24/7) f(n) for 32 ≤ n < 233. (b) If Fa is composite for all sufficiently large a, then lim infn f(2n)/f(n) ≥ 4.

Proof. (a) Take a = 5 in Theorem 4.6; the bound increases with a. (b) Every large n lies in some [2a, 2a+1), and the bound tends to 4 as a → ∞. ∎

The value 4 in (b) is the value predicted by the naive “random integer” model, which Kovač and Luca show to be misleading. Exact data give min32≤n≤606 f(2n)/f(n) = 14.26 (Section 13).

5. GROWTH ALONG CHAINS AND THE DENSITY OF GOOD N

Proposition 5.1. For all M, n ≥ 1, f(Mn) ≥ 2D(M)−2 f(n), where D(M) = maxm≤M τ(m).

Proof. Choose h ≤ M with τ(h) = D(M), and fix m ≤ n. The indices me with e ∣ h, e > 1, divide hm but not m. There are τ(h) − 1 of them, and at most one equals 6. By Lemma 2.1, T(hm) ≥ 2τ(h)−2 T(m). The numbers hm, m ≤ n, are distinct and at most Mn. Summing over m gives the claim. ∎

Theorem 5.2. Let J = J(C) = min{J : D(2J) − 2 > J log2 C}. For every n some 0 ≤ j < J satisfies f(2j+1n) > C f(2jn). Consequently {n : f(2n) > C f(n)} has lower natural density at least 1/(2J − 1). This bound is optimal for the chain property alone.

Proof. By Proposition 5.1 with M = 2J, ∏j<J f(2j+1n)/f(2jn) = f(2Jn)/f(n) ≥ 2D(2J)−2 > CJ. So some factor exceeds C.

For the density, group n ≤ x by odd part m. The chain m, 2m, 4m, … has ⌊log2(x/m)⌋ + 1 terms up to x. Every J consecutive terms contain a good one, so at least ⌊(⌊log2(x/m)⌋ + 1)/J⌋ = #{i ≥ 0 : m·2iJ+J−1 ≤ x} terms are good. Summing over odd m gives ∑i≥0 x/2iJ+J + O(J log x) = x/(2J − 1) + O(J log x). The set {n : v2(n) ≡ J − 1 (mod J)} meets every chain of J consecutive terms and has density exactly 1/(2J − 1). ∎

By direct computation, D(2J) for J = 7, 8, 12, 13, 20, 21 equals 16, 20, 48, 64, 240, 288. Hence J(4) = 8, J(16) = 13 and J(104) = 21. By (P5), log2 D(2J) = (1 + o(1)) J log 2 / log J, so J(C) = O(log log C · log log log C).

Corollary 5.3 (Kovač–Luca [10]). lim supn f(2n)/f(n) = ∞. More strongly, f(M(n)n)/f(n) → ∞ for every integer sequence M(n) → ∞.

The argument of [10] also uses highly composite numbers, through f′(n) = ∑k≤n 2τ(k). Theorem 5.2 adds the chain and density statements.

6. EXACT REDUCTION TO THIN INDICES

Definition 6.1. Fix K ≥ 2. An integer k is K-thin if its odd part has fewer than K divisors. Every divisor of a K-thin number is K-thin.

Theorem 6.2. For all n and all K ≥ 2,

∑k≤n, k not K-thin T(k) ≤ (f(2n) − f(n)) / (2K−1 − 1).

Hence f(2n)/f(n) → ∞ if and only if, for every fixed K, the T-mass of the K-thin k ≤ n is o(f(2n) − f(n)).

Proof. For k ≤ n there is a unique b ≥ 1 with N = 2bk ∈ (n, 2n]. The map k ↦ (N, b) is a bijection onto {(N, b) : N ∈ (n, 2n], 1 ≤ b ≤ v2(N)}. If k is not K-thin, then every 2ik has the same odd part u with τ(u) ≥ K. By (P4) and Lemma 2.3(b), each doubling multiplies T by at least 2K−1, so T(N) ≥ 2b(K−1) T(k). Therefore

∑k not thin T(k) ≤ ∑N∈(n,2n] T(N) ∑b≥1 2−b(K−1) = (f(2n) − f(n)) / (2K−1 − 1).

For the equivalence: if the thin mass is o(f(2n) − f(n)) for every K, then lim sup f(n)/(f(2n) − f(n)) ≤ 1/(2K−1 − 1) for every K, so the ratio tends to infinity. The converse is trivial, since the thin mass is at most f(n). ∎

Corollary 6.3 (Conditional divergence). Suppose that for every K ≥ 2,

ω(Φd(2)) = o(ψ(d)/log2 d)   (d → ∞, d K-thin).     (4)

Then f(2n)/f(n) → ∞. In particular this holds under (1), or under ω(Φd(2)) ≤ ψ(d)1−δ for some δ > 0 and all large d.

Proof. Fix K and a K-thin k ≤ n. Then τ(k) < K(log2 n + 1), and every divisor d of k is K-thin. We claim that maxd∣k ω(Φd(2)) = o(ψ(n)/log2 n), uniformly in k ≤ n. Bounded d contribute OK(1). If d ≥ √n, then log d ≥ (1/2) log n and ψ(d) ≤ ψ(n), so (4) gives the claim, uniformly because d ≥ √n → ∞. If d < √n, then (4) gives ω(Φd(2)) ≤ ψ(d) + OK(1) ≤ ψ(√n) + OK(1) = ψ(n)1/2+o(1). This is o(ψ(n)/log2 n) because ψ(n) grows faster than any power of log n. By Lemma 2.2,

log2 T(k) ≤ log2(n + 1) · K(log2 n + 1) · o(ψ(n)/log2 n) = o(ψ(n)).

So the thin mass is at most n·2o(ψ(n)) = 2o(ψ(n)). On the other hand, take m* ≤ n with τ(m*) = ψ(n) and let N be the multiple 2jm* that lies in (n, 2n]. Since 2m* − 1 ∣ 2N − 1, and by (P2), f(2n) − f(n) ≥ T(N) ≥ T(m*) ≥ 2ψ(n)−2. Theorem 6.2 now applies. The two particular cases imply (4) because log d and ψ(d)1−δ are both o(ψ(d)/log2 d). ∎

Proposition 6.4 (What a negative answer needs). Suppose f(2n) ≤ C f(n) for infinitely many n, and fix K with 2K−1 − 1 > 2C. Then for each such n there are a K-thin k ≤ n with log2 T(k) ≥ ψ(n) − 3 − log2 n, and a K-thin d ∣ k with

ω(Φd(2)) ≥ (ψ(n) − 3 − log2 n) / (K(log2 n + 1) log2(n + 1)).

Proof. By Theorem 6.2, the non-thin mass is at most (f(2n) − f(n))/(2K−1 − 1) < f(n)/2. So the thin mass is at least f(n)/2 ≥ 2ψ(n)−3, using f(n) ≥ T(m*) ≥ 2ψ(n)−2. There are at most n thin k ≤ n, which gives the first claim. The second follows from Lemma 2.2 and τ(k) < K(log2 n + 1). ∎

Proposition 6.5 (Prime spikes force near-Wagstaff Mersenne numbers). Let C, λ ≥ 1. Suppose there are infinitely many n with f(2n) ≤ C f(n) for which some prime p ≤ n satisfies T(p) ≥ f(n)/λ. Then PCλ is infinite and βCλ ≥ log 2.

Proof. Since 2p ≤ 2n, (P4) gives τ(2p + 1) = T(2p)/T(p) ≤ f(2n)/T(p) ≤ Cλ, so p ∈ PCλ. As n → ∞ we have T(p) ≥ f(n)/λ → ∞, so infinitely many distinct p occur. Next, log2 T(p) ≥ log2 f(n) − log2 λ ≥ ψ(n) − 2 − log2 λ ≥ ψ(p) − 2 − log2 λ. By Lemma 2.2, ω(Mp) ≥ (ψ(p) − 2 − log2 λ)/log2(p + 1). Hence log ω(Mp) ≥ (log 2 + o(1)) log p / log log p by (P5). ∎

So Conjecture 1.1 would rule out the simplest mechanism for a bounded ratio: a prime index carrying a fixed share of f(n). The soft-property models of Theorem 9.1 realize exactly this mechanism.

PART II. PRIME FACTORS OF 2p − 1 WHEN 2p + 1 HAS FEW DIVISORS

7. ELEMENTARY STRUCTURE

Throughout, p ≥ 5 is prime, ap = Φ2p(2) = (2p + 1)/3, ζ = ζp and 𝒪 = ℤ[ζ].

Lemma 7.1. 3 ∥ 2p + 1, so τ(2p + 1) = 2τ(ap).

Proof. ord9 2 = 6 and p is odd and prime to 3, so 2p + 1 ≡ 3 or 6 (mod 9). ∎

Lemma 7.2. (a) ap ≡ 3 (mod 8). (b) Every prime Q ∣ ap has ordQ 2 = 2p, so Q ≡ 1 (mod 2p). (c) Q ≡ 3 (mod 8) if (Q − 1)/2p is odd, and Q ≡ 1 (mod 8) otherwise. (d) The number of prime factors ≡ 3 (mod 8) of ap, counted with multiplicity, is odd.

Proof. 2p ≡ 8 (mod 24) gives (a). For (b): 2p ≡ −1 (mod Q) and Q ≠ 3 by Lemma 7.1. For (c), Euler’s criterion gives (2/Q) ≡ (2p)(Q−1)/2p ≡ (−1)(Q−1)/2p. Combine this with the parity of (Q − 1)/2 = p·(Q − 1)/2p, which fixes Q mod 4. (d) follows from (a), since 3k ≡ 3 (mod 8) if and only if k is odd. ∎

Corollary 7.3. ap is never a square. Hence 4 ∣ τ(2p + 1) and PK = P4⌊K/4⌋. In particular P6 = P7 = P4.

Lemma 7.4 (Disjoint fibres). Prime factors q of Mp have ordq 2 = p, and prime factors Q of ap have ordQ 2 = 2p. So no prime divides two members of {Mp} ∪ {ap}, and no covering-congruence argument can force ap to be composite.

Lemma 7.5 (Trivial bound). ω(Mp) ≤ (log 2/2 + o(1)) p / log p.

Proof. Let q1 < … < qk be the prime factors. They are ≡ 1 (mod 2p), so qj > 2pj and (2p)k k! < 2p. We may assume k ≥ p/log2 p. Then log k ≥ log p − 2 log log p and k! ≥ (k/e)k give k(2 log p − 2 log log p + log 2 − 1) < p log 2. ∎

Using that q lies in two classes modulo 8p and the Montgomery–Vaughan form of the Brun–Titchmarsh inequality [12], one gets the second-order form ω(Mp) ≤ (log 2/2) p / (log p − 1.0298 + o(1)). The constant log 2/2 is unchanged.

Remark 7.6 (Scale of the problem). The quantity in βK lies in [0, (1 + o(1)) log log p]. So even βK < ∞ requires ω(Mp) ≤ pO(1/log log p) on PK, far beyond Lemma 7.5. At most p log 2 / log X prime factors exceed X. So the range that must be controlled is q ∈ [p1+o(1), 2p1−o(1)], not a range with a fixed ε.

8. WHAT A DISPROOF, OR FINITENESS, WOULD MEAN

Theorem 8.1. Suppose βK > 0 and 0 < c < βK. Then there are infinitely many primes p such that Ω(ap) ≤ K/2 − 1, ω(ap) ≤ log2(K/2), and ω(Mp) ≥ exp(c log p / log log p). In particular these Mp are composite.

Proof. βK > 0 forces PK to be infinite. Apply τ(n) ≥ Ω(n) + 1 and τ(n) ≥ 2ω(n) to τ(ap) ≤ K/2. ∎

The infinitude of composite Mp with p prime is open (Guy [8], A3). A disproof would settle it together with an almost-prime statement for (2p + 1)/3 on the same set of p.

Proposition 8.2. PK is finite for every K if and only if Ω(ap) → ∞. In particular, finiteness of P4 means that there are only finitely many Wagstaff primes.

This is believed false. The Lenstra–Pomerance–Wagstaff-type heuristic (see [2]) predicts ≍ log x Wagstaff exponents p ≤ x.

Proposition 8.3 (No fixed-family certificate). Let f1, …, fr ∈ ℤ[x] be nonconstant. There is a set of primes p of positive relative density on which no fj(p) is a divisor of ap other than ±1, and likewise for Mp.

Proof. If fj = c·xk, then fj(p) = cpk is never such a divisor, because every prime factor of apMp is at least 2p + 1. Otherwise write fj = xkgj with gj(0) ≠ 0 and gj nonconstant. By Schur’s theorem infinitely many primes ℓ divide some value gj(r). Choose such odd primes ℓj, distinct and not dividing gj(0), with residues rj. Then rj ≢ 0 (mod ℓj). By the Chinese remainder theorem and Dirichlet’s theorem, a positive proportion of primes satisfy p ≡ rj (mod ℓj) for all j. For these p we have ℓj ∣ fj(p), while ℓj < 2p + 1 for large p. So fj(p) divides neither ap nor Mp, unless fj(p) = 0, which happens for finitely many p. ∎

The working note’s proof of Proposition 8.3 omitted the case fj = c·xk, in which no nonzero residue works; the repair is the first sentence above.

9. BARRIERS FOR SOFT AND BOUNDED-DEPTH ARGUMENTS

The results in this section are meta-theorems. Each lists properties of the true factorizations and builds fake factorizations with all of them but with opposite behaviour. Any argument that uses only the listed properties therefore proves nothing about the question. The constructions are explicit, but they rely on the Siegel–Walfisz or Bombieri–Vinogradov theorems and are ineffective. Full verifications are in the working notes. Here we give statements and the key steps; these results are proved in sketch form only.

9.1 Soft properties of 2k − 1.

The soft properties are the following:

  • the cyclotomic factorization and gcd(2a − 1, 2b − 1) = 2gcd(a,b) − 1;
  • a primitive prime ≡ 1 (mod d) for every index d ≠ 1, 6;
  • T(2k) = T(k) τ(2k + 1) and lifting-the-exponent multiplicities;
  • the size log Φd(2) ≈ φ(d) log 2 and Aurifeuillian splitting;
  • the rule q ≡ ±1 (mod 8) if and only if 2 ∣ (q − 1)/ordq 2, and power-residue rules of bounded depth;
  • Stewart’s lower bound for the largest prime factor [14];
  • Brun–Titchmarsh and averaged sieve bounds.

Theorem 9.1 (Models for the doubling problem; sketch). There are “fake” factorizations, in which each Φd(2) is replaced by a product of genuine primes ≡ 1 (mod d) satisfying all soft properties, of the following kind. They have one prime per generic index (two at Aurifeuillian indices). Along a sparse sequence of primes ℓj, index ℓj carries Wj ≥ D(3ℓj) + O(log ℓj) primes and index 2ℓj carries one. For these models f*(2ℓj)/f*(ℓj) → 5, so lim inf f*(2n)/f*(n) < ∞. Other models, with every ω(Φ*d(2)) ≤ do(1), have f*(2n)/f*(n) → ∞.

Key steps. In (ℓj, 2ℓj] the only multiples of ℓj are ℓj and 2ℓj, so T*(ℓj) = 2Wj and T*(2ℓj) = 4T*(ℓj). Every other k ≤ 2ℓj has log2 T*(k) ≤ D(2ℓj) + ∑i<j Wi + O(log ℓj). When the ℓj grow fast, this is negligible against Wj. Then f*(ℓj) ~ T*(ℓj) and f*(2ℓj) ~ 5T*(ℓj). Size is compatible because Wj ≪ ℓj/log ℓj primes ≡ 1 (mod ℓj) fit inside 2ℓj. Primes in the required residue classes, of modulus O(d), exist by Siegel–Walfisz. ∎

The model at index 2ℓj has Φ*2ℓj(2) “prime”, that is, τ(2ℓj + 1) = 4, and ω(M*ℓj) is huge. This is exactly the near-Wagstaff scenario of Proposition 6.5. The one property such models cannot imitate is exact divisibility: q ∣ 2p − 1 if and only if 2 is a ((q − 1)/p)-th power residue modulo q, a residue condition of unbounded depth.

9.2 Bounded-depth axioms for Mp and ap.

Definition 9.2 (Axioms AxD,J). A family assigns to each prime p ≥ 5 multisets of primes F(p) (a fake Mp) and G(p) (a fake ap) such that:

  • (A1) ∏F(p) has p bits and ∏G(p) has p − 1 bits;
  • (A2) every element is ≡ 1 (mod 2p);
  • (A3) the mod-8 rules of Lemma 7.2 and q ≡ ±1 (mod 8) for F-primes hold;
  • (A4) ∏F ≡ Mp and ∏G ≡ ap (mod 8pJ); this includes the Fermat-quotient congruence;
  • (A5) for d ≤ D with d ∣ q − 1: 2(q−1)/d ≡ 1 (mod q) if and only if d ∣ (q − 1)/p for F-primes, or d ∣ (q − 1)/2p for G-primes;
  • (A6) fibres are disjoint, and 3 ∉ G(p);
  • (A7) repeated primes are Wieferich-type;
  • (A8) Brun–Titchmarsh holds, and (A9) Stewart’s bound holds.

Theorem 9.3 (Barrier; sketch, modulo Siegel–Walfisz). The true factorizations satisfy every AxD,J. For every D, J there are families satisfying AxD,J with |G(p)| = 1 for all large p, that is, with “τ(2p + 1) = 4”, and with either (a) |F(p)| = 1 for all large p, so βK = 0; or (b) |F(p)| ≥ p1−δ/4 − 1 for all large p, so βK = ∞. A diagonal family satisfies all AxD,J at once, eventually in p.

Construction. F(p) consists of r primes (r even) with qi ≡ 1 (mod 2p), qi ≡ 7 (mod 8), and qi ≡ 2 (mod ℓ) for every odd prime ℓ ≤ D, chosen up to exp(pδ). A filler prime fixes (A1) and (A4); because r is even, the filler can be taken ≡ 7 (mod 8). The condition q ≡ 2 (mod ℓ) means that the only d ≤ D dividing q − 1 are d ∈ {1, 2}, so (A5) reduces to the quadratic character of 2, which (A3) already fixes. G(p) is a single prime in the right classes. The moduli are at most a fixed power of log x, so Siegel–Walfisz applies uniformly. ∎

Proposition 9.4 (Sharpness). (A4) with 8pJ > 2p−1 forces ∏F(p) = Mp, and adding exact divisibility ∏F ∣ Mp recovers the truth. So fake models die only at p-adic depth J ≳ p log 2 / log p, or at residue depth (q − 1)/p, which is unbounded.

Lemma 9.5 (Mod-24 limit of the construction). No product of primes that are all ≡ 7 (mod 8) and ≡ 2 (mod 3) is ≡ Mp (mod 24).

Proof. Mp ≡ 7 (mod 8) needs an odd number of factors, and Mp ≡ 1 (mod 3) needs an even number. ∎

So adding product congruences modulo 3 forces genuine Chebotarev conditions in non-abelian Kummer fields such as ℚ(ζ3, 21/3). The construction above does not handle these; that extension is unproved.

Theorem 9.6 (Barrier for the constant; sketch, using Bombieri–Vinogradov and Heath-Brown’s Linnik bound [9]). Fix ε ∈ (0, 1/10) and D ≥ 3. For all primes p outside a set of relative density zero, there are a set Q′p of at least (log 2/2 − ε) p / log p primes in [p2, p2(log p)20] and a prime r with the following properties. Each of them is ≡ 1 (mod 2p), ≡ 7 (mod 8), and ≢ 1 (mod ℓ) for odd ℓ ≤ D. The product N′ = r·∏Q′p satisfies N′ < Mp and N′ ≡ Mp (mod 2ipj) for all i ≤ εp/12, j ≤ εp/(12 log p). Brun–Titchmarsh and Stewart’s bound hold for them. So no argument that uses only these properties can improve the constant in Lemma 7.5.

Any improvement of log 2/2 is equivalent to bounding the product of the q ∣ Mp in [p2−η, p2+η] by 2(1−δ)p. This is a concentration lemma proved in the working notes. This window is Hooley’s middle range in Artin’s conjecture.

10. A GENUINE OBSTRUCTION: HONEST IDEALS WITH MANY PRIME FACTORS

The fake models violate exact divisibility. The next family does not: it consists of genuine elements of 𝒪.

Theorem 10.1. Let p ≥ 5, p ∤ k, and αk = 2k − ζ. Then:

  1. N(αk) = Φp(2k) = ∏d∣k Φdp(2) and 𝒪/(αk) ≅ ℤ/Φp(2k);
  2. for every prime q ≠ p dividing N(αk), 2k has order exactly p modulo q, and (αk) = ∏q (q, ζ − 2k)vq(N αk), a product of degree-one primes, one above each q;
  3. ω(αk) ≥ τ(k). Choosing k < p with τ(k) maximal gives log ω(αk) ≥ (log 2 + o(1)) log p / log log p.

Proof. (1) N(2k − ζ) = ∏a (2k − ζa) = Φp(2k). Since p ∤ k, Φp(xk) = ∏d∣k Φdp(x). Also 𝒪/(2k − ζ) ≅ ℤ[x]/(Φp(x), x − 2k) ≅ ℤ/Φp(2k). (2) If q ∣ Φp(2k) and 2k ≡ 1 (mod q), then Φp(2k) ≡ p (mod q), so q = p. Hence 2k has order p. Because the quotient is cyclic, exactly one prime above q divides (αk), namely (q, ζ − 2k), to the exponent vq(N αk). (3) Each Φdp(2), d ∣ k, has a primitive prime (P2), since dp ∉ {1, 6}. Distinct d give distinct primes. Finally, (P5) gives maxk<p τ(k) = 2(1+o(1)) log p / log log p. ∎

A direct computation for p = 13, k = 12 gives ω(α12) = 13 ≥ τ(12) = 6.

Remark 10.2. The structural identities used by class-group, Stickelberger, Galois-orbit and successive-minima approaches hold for αk exactly as for α1 = 2 − ζ. Examples are the sub-ideal dictionary 𝒪/I ≅ ℤ/dI, free Galois orbits of the primes, and Stickelberger relations. The only datum separating 2 − ζ from αk is quantitative: the house is 3 against 2k + 1, and log N ≈ p log 2 against kp log 2. So a proof of Conjecture 1.1 must use the size of the generator in a way stronger than the norm bound. One caveat: αk does not satisfy the analogue of p ∈ PK, since N(2k + ζ) has at least τ(odd part of k) prime factors. The coupling with 2 + ζ enters the methods tested below only through the residue 2 + ζ ≡ 4, which needs no factorization.

11. THE FUNCTION-FIELD ANALOGUE IS TRUE

For g ∈ 𝔽ℓ[T], τ(g) counts monic divisors and ω(g) counts distinct monic irreducible factors.

Theorem 11.1. Let ℓ be an odd prime, a ∈ 𝔽ℓ[T] of degree d ≥ 1, p ≠ ℓ an odd prime, and f = ordp ℓ. Then

  1. ω(ap − 1) ≤ d(1 + (p − 1)/f);
  2. ω(ap + 1) ≥ (p − 1)/f;
  3. if τ(ap + 1) ≤ K, then ω(ap − 1) ≤ d(1 + log2 K), uniformly in p.

Proof. Let μp ⊂ 𝔽ℓf be the p-th roots of unity. The Frobenius x ↦ xℓ acts on μp ∖ {1} with (p − 1)/f orbits of size f. For η ≠ 1 let N(a − η) = ∏σ (a − ησ) ∈ 𝔽ℓ[T], the product over Gal(𝔽ℓf/𝔽ℓ).

(1) ap − 1 = (a − 1) ∏orbits N(a − η). At most d primes divide a − 1. If an irreducible π divides ap − 1 but not a − 1, then a has order p in (𝔽ℓ[T]/π)×, a cyclic group of order ℓdeg π − 1. So f ∣ deg π. Each N(a − η) has degree df, so it has at most d distinct irreducible factors of degree ≥ f.

(2) ap + 1 = ∏η∈μp (a + η), since p is odd. The norms N(a + η) over distinct orbits are nonconstant. They are pairwise coprime: a common prime factor in 𝔽ℓf[T] of a + ησ and a + η′σ′ would divide the nonzero constant ησ − η′σ′.

(3) 2(p−1)/f ≤ 2ω(ap+1) ≤ τ(ap + 1) ≤ K, so (p − 1)/f ≤ log2 K; now apply (1). ∎

Remark 11.2 (Why it does not transfer). The constant-field Frobenius order f controls both ap − 1 and ap + 1. Over ℤ, every prime above q ∣ Mp and above Q ∣ ap has residue degree 1 in ℚ(ζp), so no shared invariant exists. Over ℤ, a uniform bound ω(Mp) ≤ C(K) on PK would be the literal analogue of (3). The data already strain it: p = 43, 79, 191, 701 have ap prime and ω(Mp) = 3, 3, 5, 8.

12. EXACT-DIVISIBILITY TOOLS, AND THE SINGLE OPEN ESTIMATE

Lemma 12.1 (Incidence/norm lemma). For q ∣ Mp, the primes of 𝒪 above q are 𝔮q,j = (q, ζ − 2j), 1 ≤ j ≤ p − 1. They are distinct, of degree 1, and v𝔮q,j(ζ − 2j) = vq(Mp). Consequently, for P ∈ ℤ[x] with Φp ∤ P,

∑j=1p−1 log gcd(P(2j), Mp) ≤ log |N(P(ζ))| ≤ (p − 1) log ‖P‖1.

Proof. 2 has order p modulo q, so the roots of Φp modulo q are 2j, 1 ≤ j ≤ p − 1, and q splits completely. For 1 ≤ j ≤ p − 1, N(2j − ζ) = Φp(2j). By (P3), vq(Φp(2j)) = vq(2jp − 1) = vq(Mp) + vq(j) = vq(Mp). The quotient 𝒪/(2j − ζ) is cyclic, so only 𝔮q,j above q divides (2j − ζ), to exponent vq(Mp). Hence P(ζ) ≡ P(2j) (mod 𝔮q,jvq(Mp)), and v𝔮q,j(P(ζ)) ≥ min(vq(P(2j)), vq(Mp)). The ideals 𝔮q,j are distinct of norm q, which gives the first inequality. The second is |P(ζa)| ≤ ‖P‖1. ∎

In the fake models this fails: for a fake q, (q, ζ − 2) = (1). It is the basic exact-divisibility input. Lattice-existence (Siegel, Minkowski) arguments cannot extract a bound on |S| from it, because both sides agree up to factors independent of |S|.

Lemma 12.2 (Divisor lattices). Let s > Nθ with gcd(s, N) = 1. Let a nonempty set D′ of divisors of N meet R residue classes modulo s. Then θ·ω(N) < 1 + log2 R + log2(τ(N)/|D′|).

Proof. Let t = ⌊θ·ω(N)⌋ and let A be the product of the t smallest prime-power parts of N. Then A ≤ Nt/ω(N) ≤ Nθ < s. Write N = AB. Every divisor is ab with a ∣ A, b ∣ B. If ab ≡ a′b (mod s), then a ≡ a′ because b is invertible modulo s, so a = a′ because a, a′ ≤ A < s. Hence each class contains at most τ(B) = τ(N)/τ(A) ≤ τ(N)/2t divisors. So |D′| ≤ R·τ(N)/2t, and θ·ω(N) < t + 1. ∎

A hypothesis that all divisors of Mp lie in few classes modulo some s ≥ Mp1/4+ε is what Lenstra’s theorem [11] or Coppersmith–Howgrave-Graham–Nagaraj [4] would need. By Lemma 12.2, that hypothesis already gives the conclusion up to a factor 4 in ω. So the input is the problem itself.

Proposition 12.3 (p-adic constraints are void). Write the prime factors of Mp, with multiplicity w = Ω(Mp), as qi = 1 + 2pmi. Modulo pJ the full constraint is ∏i (1 + 2pmi) ≡ Mp (mod pJ+1), one equation in the cyclic group (1 + pℤ)/(1 + pJ+1ℤ) of order pJ. Its solution set in (ℤ/pJ)w has exactly pJ(w−1) elements, a density of p−J for every w. So no number of factors is ever excluded, and the elementary symmetric functions of the mi are not individually determined.

Proof. m ↦ 1 + 2pm is a bijection from ℤ/pJ onto that group, and Mp ≡ 1 (mod p). ∎

Further tests. The working notes test four more families of methods, each with a stated point of failure.

  • Reciprocity. Every power-reciprocity law between 2 − ζ and 2 + ζ reduces to local symbols needing neither factorization. For q ∣ Mp and n ∣ q − 1 with p ∤ n, 2 is automatically an n-th power.
  • Subgroup bounds. ⟨2⟩ ⊂ 𝔽q× is the subgroup of order p. So Stepanov, Heath-Brown–Konyagin and Bourgain–Glibichuk–Konyagin bounds are identical for every q ≡ 1 (mod 2p).
  • Sparse multiples. Pigeonhole forces d ∣ ∑i≤T ±2ei for every odd d < C(p, T) (binomial coefficient). The needed uniform bound on gcd(u, Mp) for sparse u is false. For example, gcd(2 + 230 − 211 − 212, M43) = 9719 with 43 ∈ P4.
  • Kummer–Chebotarev. q ∣ Mp if and only if q splits completely in ℚ(ζm, 21/m), m = (q − 1)/p. The degree grows like (q/p)2, and each m has one candidate q.

The single open estimate. Every route above stops at the following statement:

#{q ≤ 2p : q ≡ 1 (2p), 2p ≡ 1 (q)} ≤ exp(o(log p / log log p))   (p ∈ PK or all p).     (5)

The best known bound is (log 2/2 + o(1)) p / log p. We know of no pointwise bound that is even o(p / log p). By Section 9 and Theorem 10.1, a proof of (5) must use exact divisibility of unbounded depth and the archimedean smallness of 2 − ζp beyond its norm.

The p-adic digits of Mp fix only ∑k (2p)k−1ek = (2p−1 − 1)/p, not each elementary symmetric function ek. “(2 + ζp) has at most log2(K/2) prime-ideal factors” holds for distinct factors; with multiplicity the bound is K/2 − 1.

16. OPEN PROBLEMS

  1. Is lim inf f(2n)/f(n) > 3? Is it at least 4 unconditionally, or ∞?
  2. Prove (4), or any bound ω(Φd(2)) ≤ ψ(d)1−δ on thin d.
  3. Prove ω(Mp) = o(p / log p) for all primes p, or even for a density-one set of p. By Theorem 9.6 this requires exact divisibility in Hooley’s middle range.
  4. Determine βK. A first step would be to rule out βK ≥ log 2, which by Proposition 6.5 would close the prime-spike route to a negative answer to #893.
